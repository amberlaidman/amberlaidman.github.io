The Empty Seat

What it’s actually like to ride in a car that drives itself.

[Opening film — a robotaxi pulls up to the curb in soft evening light; the door opens on an empty driver’s seat.]

A car pulls up. You open the door, climb in — and the driver’s seat is empty. No one turns around to greet you. No hands on the wheel. The car just… goes.

If that makes something in your chest tighten, you’re not alone. About half of people say they wouldn’t get in one. The empty seat is the whole reason this feels strange.

So let’s not pretend it doesn’t. We’ll look at the real thing: why these exist, how the car drives, whether it’s actually safe, and what a ride feels like. Then you can decide for yourself.

Module 2 of 6

Why This Exists

The question underneath everything else.

Before we get into how these cars work, one question worth sitting with: why build them at all?

Here’s a number most people don’t know. On average, older adults outlive their ability to drive safely by seven to ten years. Seven to ten years of needing someone else for every doctor’s visit, every grocery run, every trip to see a friend.

That’s what a car that drives itself is actually built to solve. Not novelty. Mobility — a way to get where you need to go when driving yourself isn’t an option anymore. For a lot of people, this isn’t a gadget. It’s a way to stay independent.

Module 3 of 6

Always Attentive

How a car with no driver actually drives.

Here’s what people picture: a computer “guessing” its way down the road. That’s not it. The car does the same four things you do when you drive. It just does them constantly, in every direction at once, and never checks its phone.

01/04

It senses.

A cyclist comes up on your right, half-hidden behind a parked van. Cameras, radar, and laser sensors have already placed her on the car’s map. So are the van, the curb, and the car behind you. The whole 360° picture gets rebuilt many times a second, day or night.

02/04

It predicts.

She starts drifting left to get around the van. The car doesn’t just see her — it works out where she’ll be in the next two seconds, and treats that space as already taken.

03/04

It decides.

Hold back, or steer wide? Dozens of times a second it re-runs that choice as she moves, and settles on whichever leaves her the most room.

04/04

It drives.

It eases off, lets her clear the van, then goes. Smoothly, inside the rules of the road. No impatience, no squeeze, no horn.

That’s the whole idea. Not magic, not a guess — a careful driver that never gets tired, never checks its phone, and never loses its temper.

Module 4 of 6

Is It Actually Safe?

The question that actually matters.

You’ve seen how it works. Here’s the question that actually matters: would you trust it with your life?

Start with what it’s being compared to. In 2024, more than 39,000 people died on U.S. roads. Thousands of those crashes came down to something a machine simply doesn’t do: driving tired, drunk, or distracted. That’s the status quo. So the real question isn’t whether the car is perfect. It’s whether it’s safer than that.

92%

On the roads where Waymo runs, its cars are involved in far fewer serious-injury crashes than human drivers on those same streets — 92% fewer. Not “about the same.” Not “a little better.” A different order of magnitude.

Source: Waymo, Comparison of Waymo Rider-Only Crash Data to Human Benchmarks — crash rates over 56.7 million rider-only miles measured against human-driver benchmarks on the same roads, published in Traffic Injury Prevention (2024).

And that number isn’t Waymo patting itself on the back. It comes from crash data reported to federal safety regulators. It’s measured against human drivers on those very same roads. And the method behind it is published in a peer-reviewed safety journal, open for outside researchers to check. That’s the difference between “trust us” and showing the work.

There’s a second line of evidence, from a different direction. Swiss Re — one of the world’s largest reinsurers — measured Waymo’s insurance claims against its own book of business: more than 500,000 human claims across over 200 billion miles. Over 25.3 million driverless miles, Waymo produced nine property-damage claims and two injury claims. Against newer cars with the latest driver-assistance tech, that’s roughly 90% fewer. Worth naming: Waymo co-authored that study too. But the human yardstick is the insurer’s, not Waymo’s — and a claim is a record of who was at fault, which is a harder thing to shade.

One thing the number does not mean: that it would drive that well anywhere. Those 92% are on streets Waymo has mapped down to the curb, in cities it chose, mostly in weather it handles well. That’s the honest limit of the claim — and it’s also the reassuring part. It only drives where it has already proven it can.

So why is it that much safer? Because of everything the car has to survive before it’s ever allowed to pick you up.

01/04

Millions of miles in simulation.

Before it touches a public street, the system drives endless virtual miles, rehearsing the rare, dangerous moments a human might meet once in a lifetime.

02/04

Supervised real-world testing.

Then it drives real roads with trained safety operators ready to take over — building a record in actual traffic, weather, and chaos.

03/04

One neighborhood at a time.

No citywide switch-flip. It earns each area slowly — mapped down to the curb, proven, then expanded.

04/04

Always being watched.

Every serious incident gets reported to regulators, studied, and fed back in. It never stops being graded.

Is it perfect? No — and don’t trust anyone who says it is. In October 2023 a Cruise robotaxi in San Francisco dragged a pedestrian roughly twenty feet, after a human driver struck her into its path. California pulled the permit. The fleet came off the road. In late 2025 federal regulators opened an investigation into Waymo cars driving around stopped school buses — Austin’s school district counted twenty citations in one school year. In January 2026 a Waymo struck a child near a Santa Monica elementary school. Those are real, and they’re the reason to stay curious rather than sold. But look at what followed each one. Investigated, documented in public, answered for — Waymo filed a software recall over the school-bus behavior. A technology whose every mistake gets examined in the open is being held to a standard no human driver has ever faced. The scrutiny isn’t the reason to worry. It’s the reason you can believe the number.

That’s the record, and the rigor behind it. Now the part you actually came for — what it’s like to be in the car.

Module 5 of 6

What to Expect on a Ride

The part you actually came for.

Quick context before we get in. “Self-driving” covers a lot — from cars with fancy cruise control that still need you watching the road, all the way to a car with genuinely no one driving. We mean that last one: the robotaxi. You don’t own it and you don’t drive it — you hail it like a rideshare, and it shows up empty.

Waymo is the one most people meet first, so that’s what we’ll ride through here. It isn’t the only one — Zoox is running in Las Vegas, and more are arriving in 2026 — but they all work much the same way.

The ride, step by step

01/06

Request it.

Open the app and request a ride, same as any rideshare. The app tells you when it’s about a minute out.

02/06

Spot it.

It pulls up on its own. Your initials appear on the dome on the car’s roof, so you know it’s yours.

03/06

Get in.

The car arrives locked — unlock it from the app, then open the door and climb in the back. The front seat’s empty — that’s normal now.

04/06

Start.

Buckle up and tap “Start ride” on the in-car screen. The car won’t move until you’re set.

05/06

During.

Sit back. A screen shows you what the car sees in real time. Music, temperature, and windows are yours. Need to stop early? Tap “Pull over” anytime.

06/06

Arrive.

It pulls over and parks. Open the doors from the screen, step out — and close the door behind you.

Inside the cabin, point by point

  • The screen.

    The in-car touchscreen shows your route and runs your trip — start the ride, pull over, play music, adjust the temperature, and reach Support.

  • Support.

    Tap Support on the screen or in the app any time, day or night, to reach a real person.

  • The empty driver’s seat.

    No one sits up front to drive. The driver’s seat stays empty the whole trip — and after a few minutes, you stop noticing.

  • Seatbelt.

    Buckle up before you start. Seatbelts are required for everyone, the whole ride.

  • Doors.

    The car arrives locked. Unlock from the app or the screen, then open the door. At your stop, open the doors from the screen — and remember to close them behind you.

What if something goes wrong?

Your call

You’re six minutes into the ride. The car slows down, signals, and pulls over to the curb — nowhere near your stop. The screen says it’s working on it. What do you do?

A · Get out and walk — something’s clearly wrong.

B · Wait, and tap Support on the screen if it doesn’t move.

C · Try to override it from the screen and make it keep driving.

B. Pulling over is what the car does when it isn’t sure — usually it resolves in seconds. If it doesn’t, Support is one tap away, and they can see what the car sees. You could get out (the doors unlock and you’re never trapped), but you’d be leaving a ride that was about to continue. And there’s nothing to override — that’s deliberate. You can end the ride or reach a person, but you can’t make it drive into something it’s unsure about.

The honest question everyone has: what if the car gets confused, or something just feels off?

It’s built for exactly that. If the car hits a situation it’s unsure about, it does the safest thing — it slows or pulls over, instead of guessing. It can reach a remote support team who see what the car sees and help it read a tricky scene. They don’t drive it by remote control — the car stays in charge; they just help it understand. And you’re never stuck: one tap reaches a real person, any time, for any reason.

Empty seat, sure. But you’re not alone — and the car’s first instinct when in doubt is to stop, not to gamble.

Remember what made you hesitate at the start? The empty seat? It’s still empty. But now you know what’s behind it. A careful driver that never looks away. A safety record on its streets that beats human drivers. And a ride that’s honestly kind of ordinary once you’re in it.

You don’t have to love the idea. You just have a clearer picture to decide with. So — which one sounds like you?

“I could actually take one”

Then maybe today’s the day. There’s likely a robotaxi near you, or one coming soon — and the honest truth is, the first ride is the hardest part, because it’s all anticipation. Download the service’s app, set your pickup and destination like any rideshare, and go. Most people feel the nerves melt somewhere in the first few minutes — and by the second ride, they forget it was ever a question.

“Not where I am — yet”

That’s most people right now, and that’s completely fine. It’s coming — the map is filling in fast. When a service reaches your city, you can usually join a waitlist or follow updates so you’re not caught off guard. And when your turn comes, you won’t be starting from scratch — you’ll already know what the seat looks like, how the ride feels, and why that empty front seat isn’t the thing to worry about.

Appendix

Things Worth Remembering

Not a set of instructions — the buttons differ by service and change often. These are the things that hold true whichever car pulls up.

Every operator does the mechanics slightly differently, and any of them can redesign an app between now and your first ride. So this is deliberately not a walkthrough. It is the handful of things that stay true, and that are worth carrying with you.

Remember, before you go

—Coverage is local, and often only part of a city. Whether a service runs where you are is the first question, not the last — and the answer changes month to month.
—Everything you need to set up — account, payment, permissions — is easier to do at home than standing at a curb.
—Pickup and dropoff points can be restricted. Worth knowing before you are counting on one.

Remember, when the car arrives

—You will be given a way to confirm this is your car — a plate number, a name on a screen, a light. Use it before you get in.
—The car may not stop exactly where you are standing. Expect a short walk, and expect the app to tell you where to go.
—It will not wait indefinitely. There is a countdown, usually a few minutes and shorter on a busy street, and missing it can mean a fee and a fresh request. Be at the curb before it arrives, not after.

Remember, during the ride

—The driving is deliberately cautious and defensive. It brakes earlier than a person would, and that is the intent rather than a fault.
—When it is unsure, it slows or stops rather than guessing. Sometimes it just needs a moment to work out how to proceed, and then carries on by itself.
—You are not expected to do anything, and you should not touch the wheel or the pedals. Once you are in, the pace is yours — the ride does not begin until you begin it.
—There will always be a way to reach a real person, and a way to end the ride early. Find both before you need them — usually on the screen in front of you or in the app.

Remember, at the end

—The doors are yours to open and yours to close.
—Check the seat before you walk away. Most services will flag a forgotten item, but not all of them will.
—It ends and charges on its own. Nothing to sign, and no driver to tip.

For the actual buttons, go to the source

—Whichever service you use publishes its own rider guidance, and it is kept current in a way this document cannot be. Read it once before a first ride.
—Waymo, as an example of what to look for, covers first trips, riding tips, and what happens if a ride is interrupted: Your first trip, Riding tips, Ride interruptions. Current cities are on the FAQ.
—Other operators publish equivalents. The shape of the ride is the same everywhere. The buttons are not.

If you read this and the answer is still no, that is a legitimate answer. Knowing exactly what you are declining is worth something on its own.
